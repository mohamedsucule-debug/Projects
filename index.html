Attention

45,440 parameters, trained with an automatic differentiation engine written from nothing — no PyTorch, no library at all, every backward pass checked against finite differences. Drag through training and watch eight attention heads go from an even smear to bright stripes at the exact step the loss falls off a cliff. The circuit that appears is the induction head, and it lands in the layer the theory says it must.

Agents

The tool-use loop is ten lines and everybody writes it correctly. Everything hard is what happens when a tool throws, a result is five kilobytes, or the model calls the same thing four times. Step through a real loop over a real ledger, then switch the guards off one at a time and watch what each one was holding back.

If you only read one thing: the two bugs in the Attention write-up. The first made a positional copy head look exactly like an induction head; the second made one induction head look like eight different ones. Both produced a plausible picture and a beautiful number, and neither would ever have thrown. Finding them took longer than the build.

Covers — a restaurant floor and booking system

You arrive at 19:42 on a Saturday. Thirty-one bookings are in the book, eleven parties are eating, three groups are waiting at the bar, and there is a double-booking on table 2 that somebody needs to sort out before half past eight.

Drag a party onto a table and it tells you whether they fit — and when they do not, why not, and what to do instead.

  • “Yes — if one booking moves.” It searches the night for a rearrangement that turns a no into a table, and shows you exactly who it would shift
  • Re-plan the whole evening in one click, see what it buys before anything happens, then watch the moves fly across the floor
  • Open it in two windows and they stay in step — because a restaurant has more than one screen
  • No backend, no database, no signup — and sixty-seven tests on the rules alone

The newest one, and the one people ask for by name

Gobl — Instagram for food, with a search that works

Twenty-two restaurants, ninety-five reviews and eight tribes. Filter by cuisine, neighbourhood, price, meal, queue, noise and what a kitchen can actually guarantee a vegan — then see what people thought, metric by metric, including where they disagreed.

Every restaurant app ships the same three bugs. This one is built to not have them, and each one has a test named after it.

  • A five from two people is not a five. Every average is pulled towards the site average, so a rating has to be earned by volume before it can top a list
  • Filter counts that stay useful — the number next to every option is what you would get if you picked it, checked by picking it
  • “Vegan” reads the kitchen, never the caption — because “not great for vegans” contains the word, and somebody gets ill
  • No backend and no image files: every plate on screen is drawn from the dish id

How I work

I build fast and I am hard to satisfy. With modern tooling a working version exists in minutes, so the job stops being typing and starts being judgement — what is worth building, what is wrong with it, whether it is actually doing what it appears to be doing, and what it should say. That is where my time goes, and it is the part that never gets easier.

build

Something running beats something planned

I would rather have a thing I can click that is wrong than a document that is right. Getting to a working version fast is the quickest way to find out what I actually wanted — which is reliably not what I wrote down at the start.

review

Looking at it beats testing it

Every screen here was rendered and inspected after each change. That is how the hourglass that leaked sand, the forest fire that burned one tree and gave up, and the island that came out as a featureless green blob all got caught. No test would have flagged any of them — they were all technically working.

verify

A beautiful result is not the same as a correct one

The transformer here learned to copy, the loss fell off a cliff on cue, and a head hit a score of 0.92. It was the wrong mechanism — it had memorised a position, not learned to match on content, and on screen the two are identical. Finding that out took longer than the original build and is the only reason the claim is worth anything. Every strong result in this repo has a check attached that would catch it being the wrong one.

write

The words are half the product

“Pour sand. Add water. Then set it on fire” teaches the sandbox faster than any tutorial could. Deciding what a thing says — and what it refuses to say — is part of building it, not a coat of paint applied at the end.
