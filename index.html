§ 1Inside the modelsfour figures

How language models actually work

Four pieces built from the algorithm up rather than from an API call. A tokeniser that trains itself in front of you; a transformer trained here with backpropagation written from nothing; the statistics that decide whether a change to one of these systems did anything at all; and an agent loop you can break on purpose. No key, no server, nothing sent anywhere — same promise as everything else here.

trained from scratch scored 0.92, and was the wrong mechanism — see E1

Attentionopen →

45,440 parameters, trained with an automatic differentiation engine written from nothing — no PyTorch, no library at all, every backward pass checked against finite differences. Drag through training and watch eight attention heads go from an even smear to bright stripes at the exact step the loss falls off a cliff. The circuit that appears is the induction head, and it lands in the layer the theory says it must.

train it yourself strawberry = 10 letters, 6 tokens

Tokensopen →

A real byte-pair encoder, trained in the page in 380ms — watch the vocabulary build itself one merge at a time. Then why a model cannot count the r's in strawberry, why arithmetic was hard and got easier, and why the same sentence costs three times as much in Japanese.

the one nobody builds at n = 100 the better one loses

Evalsopen →

Two real parsers, eight hundred real inputs, graded live. Drag the sample size and watch the confidence interval cross zero. At a hundred items the better parser scores worse — which is what three points of noise looks like when you can drag it.

break it on purpose 5.3× the tokens, same answer

Agentsopen →

The tool-use loop is ten lines and everybody writes it correctly. Everything hard is what happens when a tool throws, a result is five kilobytes, or the model calls the same thing four times. Step through a real loop over a real ledger, then switch the guards off one at a time and watch what each one was holding back.

N.B. If you only read one thing, read the two bugs in the Attention write-up. The first made a positional copy head look exactly like an induction head; the second made one induction head look like eight different ones. Both produced a plausible picture and a beautiful number, and neither would ever have thrown. Finding them took longer than the build.

§ 4The serious onesone figure, six entries

Systems that refuse to be simple

Same approach, harder subjects, and these take longer than ten seconds to get into. Each one puts something normally invisible on screen — a night's bookings, what a database is thinking, why servers disagree — and lets you poke it. You do not need to know the subject: every one opens by explaining what you are looking at.

Covers — a restaurant floor and booking system

You arrive at 19:42 on a Saturday. Thirty-one bookings are in the book, eleven parties are eating, three groups are waiting at the bar, and there is a double-booking on table 2 that somebody needs to sort out before half past eight.

Drag a party onto a table and it tells you whether they fit — and when they do not, why not, and what to do instead.

  • “Yes — if one booking moves.” It searches the night for a rearrangement that turns a no into a table, and shows you exactly who it would shift
  • Re-plan the whole evening in one click, see what it buys before anything happens, then watch the moves fly across the floor
  • Open it in two windows and they stay in step — because a restaurant has more than one screen
  • No backend, no database, no signup — and sixty-seven tests on the rules alone

    § 5Errataten corrections

    Everything here that was wrong

    I build fast and I am hard to satisfy. With modern tooling a working version exists in minutes, so the job stops being typing and starts being judgement — what is worth building, what is wrong with it, and whether it is actually doing what it appears to be doing. Every one of these was technically working code. Not one of them threw.

    1. E1

      Attention · §1

      For an induction head, scoring 0.92 read a positional copy head

      It had memorised “attend to i − 31” off the positional embeddings and never looked at a token. The period is now drawn fresh for every sequence, and the model is scored across all of them.

      Caught by distrusting a beautiful number

    2. E2

      Attention · §1

      For a head that works better at longer periods read a bad measurement

      The score only counted the nearest earlier match, so at period 12 it saw about a fifth of the head’s attention. Fixed, the spread across periods fell from 0.437 to 0.132: one head, matching on content.

      Caught by asking why the curve was so smooth

    3. E3

      Sandbox · §3

      For an hourglass read an X

      It leaked sand out of its sides, because it had been drawn as two crossed lines rather than a funnel.

      Caught by looking at it

    4. E4

      Sandbox · §3

      For a forest fire read one tree

      It burned one tree and went out. The match had been lit on an isolated tree at the edge of the map.

      Caught by looking at it

    5. E5

      Islandsmith · §3

      For a new island read a smooth green ellipse

      Every one of them. The noise frequency was so low that the entire map sat inside a single noise cell.

      Caught by looking at it

    6. E6

      Loom · §3

      For Contrast read Brightness

      The knob applied a gamma curve, so turning it up raised the mean from 0.501 to 0.587 instead of spreading the values out — the opposite of what its own label promised.

      Caught by looking at it, then measuring it

    7. E7

      Portrait · §3

      For six thousand dots read six thousand dots under a pixel wide

      The radius was divided by a canvas scale that had already been applied. The data was right the whole time; it just could not be seen.

      Caught by looking at it

    8. E8

      Morph · §3

      For in front of its backdrop read behind it

      The container carries perspective for the 3D layouts, which makes a stacking context — and no z-index inside one can lift a child above something outside it.

      Caught by looking at it
