Evals knowing whether the number moved

Your eval said 92%. The old one said 89%. You have learned nothing.

Three points on a hundred items is inside the noise, and you can show that in about fifteen lines of resampling. Almost nobody does. The demo gets built, the prompt gets tuned, the number goes up, and the thing ships on a difference that would have gone the other way if the items had arrived in a different order.

Everything below is computed live from two real parsers running on eight hundred real inputs. No simulated models, no invented scores. The better parser is genuinely better — and at a hundred items it comes out worse.

One · Something real to measure

Pull the total off a line of a receipt. It looks trivial and it is not, because of the long tail: a hundred lines are $1,234.56 and then one is 1.234,56 EUR, which is the same amount written by someone in Germany. v1 strips the commas and reads that as one euro twenty-three. v2 works out what the separators mean first — and arrives, as real improvements do, with a regression attached.

Two · How many items did you run?

Drag it. The systems do not change and the items do not change — only how many of them you looked at. Watch the interval on the difference, and watch it cross zero.

Three · Compare on the same items

Both systems saw the same inputs, so resample the items — carrying both outcomes with each one — rather than the two scores separately. An item that is hard for everybody then contributes nothing to the uncertainty about the difference, because it cancels. This is free precision, and it is thrown away by every report that shows two intervals side by side and invites you to compare them by eye.

Four · Your eval is deciding on far fewer items than you think

Items that both systems get right, and items both get wrong, carry no information at all about which is better. Only the disagreements do. So an eight-hundred-item eval comparing two similar systems is really deciding on the handful of rows where they differ — and McNemar's test is the one that says so out loud.

Five · The average hid a regression

v2 is better overall. It is also strictly worse on one input shape, and no summary number will ever tell you that. Break the score down by the kind of input and the fix and the breakage are both visible immediately — which is why a single headline metric is a reporting format, not an evaluation.

Six · Work it out before you run it, not after

The most useful number in evaluation is the one you compute before spending anything: how many items it takes to see the change you are hoping for. If the answer is more than you are willing to run, you have learned that now rather than after a week of arguing about a dashboard.

Two real parsers, eight hundred generated-but-deterministic inputs, graded live in this page — no server, no API key, nothing sent anywhere. The statistics are in stats.js (percentile bootstrap, paired and unpaired; Wilson intervals; McNemar's exact test; two-proportion power) and the task is in suite.js. 44 tests, including a coverage check that the 95% interval really does contain the truth about 95% of the time.
