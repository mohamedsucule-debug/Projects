Tokens what a model actually reads

A model has never seen a letter in its life.

It reads tokens: a few thousand common fragments of text, each one an opaque number. Almost everything people find strange about what these systems can and cannot do — that they miscount the letters in a word, that they are worse at arithmetic than a calculator from 1974, that the same sentence costs three times as much in Japanese — comes out of one algorithm, and it is small enough to fit on this page.

So here it is, running. Train it, then type into it, then look at what it does to three things you have probably already been annoyed by.

One · The vocabulary builds itself

Byte-pair encoding starts with the 256 byte values and nothing else. It counts every adjacent pair in the corpus, merges the most common one into a new token, and repeats. That is the entire algorithm. Press the button and watch the order it learns things in — pairs of letters first, then endings, then whole common words with their leading space.

Two · Type anything

Every coloured block is one token — one number, as far as the model is concerned. A block with a dashed outline is not even a whole character: it is one byte of a multi-byte one, which is what happens to any script the tokeniser was not trained on.

Three · Why it cannot count the r's

This is the famous one, and the explanation is not that the model is stupid. Nothing in it ever sees the letters. It sees two or three numbers, and the spelling of a token is a fact it has to have learned about a number — which it can sometimes do, from having read people discussing spelling, and therefore does unreliably rather than never.

Four · Why arithmetic was hard, and then got easier

GPT-2's pre-tokeniser let a run of digits be any length, so BPE was free to learn 2024 as a single token if it saw it often enough. GPT-4 capped digit runs at three, which makes a four-digit token unlearnable — for any corpus, forever. Same algorithm, one number changed in a regular expression.

Trained on this repository's prose what you are using above

The two tokenisers agree on every number. That is not the demonstration failing; it is the first half of it. No number appears often enough in this corpus to be worth a token, so both fall back to one token per digit and the pre-tokeniser has nothing to decide. What GPT-2 does with a number depends entirely on what it happened to read.

Trained on a corpus full of dates the same code, different reading

Now give both the same thousand log lines stamped with years. GPT-2 immediately spends a token on 2024 as a whole unit. GPT-4 never can, however many times it sees it — the best it manages is two pieces, because a chunk is three digits and that is that.

Look at what GPT-2 did to 1999. It came out as 1·99·9 — the middle token is the 99 out of the middle of a year, and it means nothing arithmetically. That is the real cost: not that numbers take more tokens, but that the pieces are whatever the corpus made common, so the same digit sits in a different-shaped token depending on what is around it, and a rule about carrying has to be learned separately for each shape.

GPT-4 is not immune to this — it learned 202 here and splits 2024 as 202·4. What it has is a ceiling: three digits, never more, so an n-digit number is always at least ⌈n/3⌉ pieces and the alignment is forced by the regular expression rather than by what happened to be popular. Bounded beats optimal here.

Five · The language tax

The same sentence, in several languages. The bars are tokens. Because the vocabulary was learned from English, everything else falls back towards one token per byte — and a character outside ASCII is two to four bytes before anything has even started. Everyone pays per token. Not everyone is charged the same amount for the same sentence.

This is not a rounding error. It is a longer wait, a smaller usable context window and a larger bill, for the same meaning, imposed on whoever does not write in the language the vocabulary was fitted to.
